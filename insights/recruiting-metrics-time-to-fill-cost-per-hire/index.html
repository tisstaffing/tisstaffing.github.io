Recruiting Metrics: Time-to-Fill, Cost-per-Hire and Quality-of-Hire Explained

Speed, spend, outcome and persuasion are four different questions. What each recruiting metric hides, how it gets gamed, and why a handful of numbers beats a dashboard nobody reads.

TIS Staffing

Recruitment Team

No single recruiting metric tells you whether hiring is working. Time-to-fill measures speed: how long the seat sat empty. Cost-per-hire measures spend: what it took to get someone in it. Quality-of-hire measures whether the person actually worked out. Offer-acceptance rate measures whether your process convinces good candidates to say yes. Read together, those four show where the process is leaking — a slow loop, an expensive source, a brief that oversells, or a bar set in the wrong place. Read alone, each one can be improved without improving hiring at all. That is the whole argument, and the rest of this page is about reading them properly.

Why one metric is always a bad management tool

Metrics do two jobs at once. They tell you what is happening, and they tell the people being measured what to optimise. The moment a number becomes the target, effort moves toward the number rather than the outcome behind it. A team judged on speed will hire faster. A team judged on cost will hire cheaper. Neither of those is the same as hiring well.

So the useful question is not which metric matters most — it is which combination of metrics makes gaming difficult. A number that can be improved without anything real changing is decoration. A pair of numbers that pull against each other is a control.

How to calculate each metric in plain terms

Time-to-fill

Days from the date the role was approved to the date an offer was accepted. Some teams measure to the start date instead; choose one and keep it, because mixing the two over time produces a trend line that is not measuring anything.

Two decisions change the number more than anything you do to the process. First, when the clock starts — from headcount approval, from the advert going live, or from the first interview. Approval is the honest start, because the empty seat costs money from the day it is approved, not from the day someone gets round to advertising. Second, what counts as filled — offer accepted or first day worked. Offer accepted flatters the number when notice periods are long.

Report it by role family rather than as one company-wide average. An executive search and a payroll administrator are not the same measurement, and averaging them produces a figure that describes neither.

Cost-per-hire

Total cost of filling the role divided by the number of hires. The formula is trivial; the argument is always about what counts as cost.

The narrow version — agency fee or job-board spend — is easy to produce and almost meaningless. The version worth reporting includes agency and advertising spend, recruiter time (including internal hours, costed honestly), referral bonuses, assessment tools, and any travel or relocation. It also includes the cost of roles that were never filled by that route, which is why cost-per-hire is best reported per channel and per role family rather than as a single company number.

One deliberate exclusion: do not load the hire's salary into cost-per-hire. Salary is the cost of employing someone, not the cost of finding them. Mixing them makes a senior hire look like a failure of process and a junior hire look like a triumph, which is not a useful comparison.

Quality-of-hire

There is no clean formula, and products that claim to produce one are usually scoring the interview rather than the hire. The honest definition is whether the person is still performing in the role a year later, and whether the manager would hire them again knowing what they know now.

A workable version for most teams: at twelve months, record whether the person is still in role, whether they met the outcomes written into the brief, and a simple manager judgement — would you hire them again. Three fields. Not a survey.

Offer-acceptance rate

Offers accepted divided by offers made. Track the declines separately, with a reason attached: compensation, a competing offer, the role changing during the process, or the candidate withdrawing because the process was slow or unclear. The rate is the headline; the reasons are the actual information.

What each metric hides, and how it gets gamed

Time-to-fill rewards lowering the bar

The fastest way to improve time-to-fill is to hire the first acceptable candidate, or to write a brief so loose that almost anyone clears it. Both improve the number and damage the outcome, and the damage arrives nine months later in a performance conversation or a resignation.

It also hides where the time actually goes. A search that takes eight weeks might be four weeks of market work and four weeks of a hiring manager who cannot find a slot for a second interview. Only measuring the total tells you nothing about which half to fix, so break the number down: brief to shortlist, shortlist to decision, decision to accepted offer.

Cost-per-hire punishes investment

Cost-per-hire is the metric most easily improved by making hiring worse. Spend less on sourcing, use a cheaper channel, skip the assessment, and the number falls. It also discourages the things that genuinely reduce total cost — a slower, better-qualified hire that stays for four years is cheaper than a fast cheap one that leaves in six months, but only one of those shows up in this metric.

The comparison that matters is cost per successful hire, not cost per hire. Add a second number alongside it: placements still in role at twelve months. A channel that produces the cheapest hires and the worst retention is expensive, whatever the arithmetic says.

Offer acceptance hides the candidates you never reached

A 95% acceptance rate looks excellent until you notice it was achieved by only offering to candidates who were certain to say yes. Acceptance rate says nothing about the quality of the pool, and it is silent about the candidates who withdrew before an offer existed — usually over process, timeline or pay clarity.

Which is why declines need reasons and withdrawals need counting. A candidate who leaves at second-interview stage because the process took a month to schedule was lost to the same problem as a declined offer, and the acceptance rate will never show it.

Quality-of-hire arrives too late to change the decision

Twelve-month quality data is retrospective. It tells you how a past brief, interview loop or channel performed — it cannot rescue the hire you are making this month. Treat it as calibration rather than control: the number you check to find out whether your interview process is actually predicting performance.

The practical use is comparison. If hires from one channel or one interviewer consistently perform and others do not, that is a signal worth acting on. If everything in your data looks identical, the process is probably not distinguishing between candidates at all.

Why twelve-month retention is the most honest quality signal

Twelve months is long enough that early enthusiasm has worn off and the person has had to do the job through at least one full cycle — a year-end close, a product launch, a hiring round. Someone still in role, still performing and still wanted at that point is a hire that worked. Someone gone is not, however good the interview was.

It is also hard to game in the way other metrics are. You can move a shortlist, you can compress a loop, you can negotiate a fee down. You cannot retrospectively make someone succeed in a role they left.

The honest caveats: it attributes outcomes to recruiting that are often caused by management, workload or pay, and it moves slowly, so it cannot be the only number on the page. Pair it with the reasons people left — role not as described, manager, compensation, scope — because those reasons are what tell you which part of the process to change. A hire who leaves because the role was misrepresented is a recruiting failure. A hire who leaves because the team was restructured may not be.

Retention is also where the work after the offer shows up. The first ninety days are decided by how the brief was written and how onboarding was run, which is the argument in the first 90 days decide everything.

The fee model changes what cost-per-hire means

Cost-per-hire comparisons between an agency hire and a direct hire are usually wrong, because the two numbers do not contain the same things.

An agency fee is visible and arrives as one invoice: typically a percentage of first-year salary for a permanent hire, or an hourly rate with a markup built in for contract work, as set out in how staffing agency fees work. The direct-hire alternative is spread across salaries for internal recruiters, job-board subscriptions, referral bonuses and — the part nobody costs — the management hours spent screening.

A fair comparison puts both on the same basis: cost per placement that survived twelve months, including the cost of any re-run. A cheap hire that had to be repeated is the most expensive line in the budget, and it never appears in a cost-per-hire figure that only counts first attempts.

The model also changes the incentive. A contingent fee rewards speed to placement. A retained search, usual for senior appointments and described in how executive search works, spreads the fee across the search so the partner is paid to map the market and challenge the brief rather than to close the first acceptable candidate.

Source-of-hire: the metric that tells you where to spend next

For each hire, record one primary source: referral, agency, inbound application, outbound sourcing, or internal move. Then read it alongside retention rather than on its own. A channel that produces a third of your hires and half your twelve-month leavers is not a productive channel, however good the volume looks.

Two ways this number lies. It gets assigned at the last touch — the candidate who came from a referral, went quiet, and was re-engaged by an agency gets recorded as an agency hire. And it rewards whoever got there first rather than whoever did the work. Fix both by recording the first source and defining the rule in writing before you need it.

Set a baseline before you set a target

The temptation is to adopt a target from somewhere else — a benchmark, a conference slide, a competitor. It rarely helps. Your time-to-fill depends on your market, your seniority mix and how quickly your own hiring managers respond. Your cost-per-hire depends on what you count and what your internal time is worth.

So measure for a quarter without a target. Collect the four numbers per role family, add the reasons behind the declines and the leavers, and look at the distribution rather than the average — a median plus the slowest quartile is more useful than a mean dragged around by one executive search.

Then set one target, in the place where the data shows the worst leak. If most of the delay sits between shortlist and decision, the target belongs there and it belongs to the hiring manager, not the recruiter. A target on a number nobody controls changes nothing except the reporting.

Measure external partners on the same numbers

If you hold an agency to a different standard than your internal team, you cannot compare the two, and you will end up arguing about fees instead of outcomes.

Agree four things before the search starts: the definition of a qualified shortlist, the timeline for that shortlist, how the fee interacts with a hire who leaves inside the guarantee window, and who owns the role if the consultant changes. Then measure the partner on the same cost-per-successful-hire and twelve-month retention basis you use internally.

One number worth asking for that most agencies do not volunteer: how many of their placements are still in role at twelve months, and how they define it. A partner who can answer precisely is measuring their own work. A partner who cannot is measuring their invoicing.

A scorecard of five numbers, not a dashboard

Recruiting dashboards fail for a predictable reason: they show twenty numbers, nobody knows which three matter this month, and the meeting becomes a tour of the charts. Five numbers, reviewed monthly with a named owner for each, will outperform any dashboard.

A workable set: median time-to-fill and the slowest quartile, by role family. Cost per successful hire, including re-runs. Twelve-month retention with the top leaving reason. Offer-acceptance rate with decline reasons. Source-of-hire read against retention.

Five numbers, four questions: are we fast enough, are we spending sensibly, are the people working out, and are we convincing the ones we want. If a metric does not answer one of those, it belongs in a spreadsheet someone opens when a question comes up — not on the page everyone stares at monthly.

Common mistakes

Reporting one company-wide average across wildly different role families. Starting the time-to-fill clock on the day the advert went live, which hides the delay before it. Leaving internal recruiter time out of cost-per-hire and then comparing that number to an agency fee. Treating a high acceptance rate as proof of a good process. Collecting quality-of-hire data and never reviewing it with the people who wrote the briefs. Chasing a published benchmark instead of your own baseline. And changing the definition of a metric mid-year, which turns a trend into a rumour.
