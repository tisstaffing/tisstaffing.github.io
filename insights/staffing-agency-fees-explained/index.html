How Much Do Staffing Agencies Charge in 2026?

A plain-English guide to staffing agency fees — direct hire, temp markups and retained search, plus how to tell whether an agency is really worth the rate.

Priya Raman

Head of Finance Practice

If you are hiring in 2026, one question tends to arrive before the first CV does: what is this actually going to cost? Staffing agency fees are not a single number. They move with the role, the seniority and the amount of risk an agency is willing to carry on your behalf. This guide explains how the fees work, what you should expect to pay, and how to judge whether an agency is earning its rate.

The three main fee structures

Almost every quote you receive fits one of three models. Knowing which one you are looking at is the fastest way to compare agencies fairly.

Contingent and direct-hire fees

This is the most common model for permanent roles. The agency is paid a percentage of the successful candidate first-year salary, and you only pay once someone has started. Contingent fees typically land between 15% and 25% of base salary, though they climb higher for hard-to-fill specialist roles. Because the agency is not paid unless it places someone, it usually works several searches at once and moves quickly.

Temp and contract markups

For temporary, interim and contract work there is rarely a single fee. Instead the agency bills an hourly rate that already includes the worker pay plus a markup. That markup — commonly 25% to 75% — covers employer payroll taxes, insurance, benefits and the agency margin. The range is wide because a two-week warehouse shift and a senior interim finance director carry very different overheads.

Retained search

Retained search is the norm for senior and executive hiring. You pay a retainer in stages — often a third when the search begins, a third at the shortlist stage and a third on placement — and the total usually runs 25% to 33% of first-year compensation. The higher cost buys a dedicated consultant, a confidential market mapping exercise and a guaranteed level of commitment to your search.

Typical percentage ranges for 2026

Every search is different, but these benchmarks are a fair starting point when you are budgeting.

Mid-level permanent roles generally sit at 15% to 20% of first-year salary. Specialist roles in fields such as technology and finance tend to run 20% to 25%. Senior and executive appointments climb to 25% to 33%. Temporary and contract placements are not quoted as a percentage at all — they carry an hourly markup of roughly 25% to 75%.

What the fee actually pays for

A fair fee is not commission for forwarding CVs. It pays for the work that keeps a hire from going wrong, and any agency worth its rate should be able to explain exactly what you are buying.

Sourcing is the visible part: building a shortlist from people who are not necessarily looking. Screening goes deeper, testing skills, motivation and fit before a candidate reaches your desk. Reference checks confirm that what a candidate claims holds up. And a placement guarantee gives you a defined window — usually 60 to 90 days — in which a replacement is found at no extra cost if the hire does not work out.

Why fees vary by role type

The harder a role is to fill, the more a sensible agency will charge. A technology search for a scarce engineering specialism takes longer and reaches a smaller pool of candidates than a general operations hire, so it carries a higher percentage. Our technology practice and finance practice both work in candidate-short markets, which is reflected in how their fees are structured.

Executive roles are priced differently again. A retained executive search is less about volume and more about discretion, market mapping and persuasion, so it is quoted as a fixed staged fee rather than a contingent percentage. Operations, admin and payroll roles, by contrast, usually attract the lowest permanent fees because the candidate pool is deeper.

How to tell whether an agency is worth it

A cheap fee is not the same as a good one. A low rate that produces a weak shortlist costs more in management time than a higher rate that fills the role in ten days. Ask what the fee includes, how long the guarantee runs and how the consultant will measure success. Look for an agency that pushes back on an unrealistic brief rather than simply agreeing and billing you later.

The clearest signal is a shortlist you actually want to interview. If the first three candidates are relevant and interested, the fee is doing its job. If you are doing the agency work for it, it is not.
