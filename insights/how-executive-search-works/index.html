How Does Executive Search Work? A Guide for Hiring Leaders

Executive search is a targeted, retained process for senior roles. Here is how it works, what to expect, and why honest pushback on the brief matters most.

TIS Staffing

Executive Search Team

Executive search is a targeted, usually retained process for filling senior leadership roles. Instead of posting an advert and waiting for applications, a search firm researches the market, identifies the leaders who could do the job, approaches them directly — including people who are not looking to move — and manages the process from first conversation through offer and onboarding. The employer pays a staged retainer rather than a fee on placement. This guide explains how that process works in practice, and what a hiring leader needs to bring to it.

How executive search differs from a job posting or contingency recruiting

Advertising works when there is a deep pool of candidates who are actively looking. Senior roles rarely fit that description. The strongest leaders for a given job are usually already employed, already well paid and not scanning job boards. A job ad reaches the people who happen to be looking; executive search reaches the people who should be.

Proactive outreach instead of inbound applications

A search firm builds a target list and approaches each candidate personally, explaining why the role might be worth their attention. That conversation, done well, is often what turns a passive leader into a serious candidate.

Market mapping instead of a database search

Before approaching anyone, a good consultant maps the market: which companies do this work, which leaders own it, and how the reporting lines and remits compare. That map becomes the basis for a shortlist you could not have assembled from a CV database.

Discretion by default

Senior searches often replace an incumbent, open a new function or signal a strategic move. Search is run discreetly so the market does not learn more than you intend, and so candidates can explore a role without signalling to their current employer.

The stages of an executive search

1. Position definition

The search starts with the brief, not the shortlist. A consultant will push you to define what success looks like in the role, the scope and reporting line, and the trade-offs you are willing to make. Getting this right early prevents a shortlist that is technically qualified and completely wrong.

2. Market mapping and sourcing

With the brief agreed, the consultant maps the market, builds a long list of possible candidates and approaches the strongest directly. This is where a search earns its fee: reaching people who would never have replied to an advert.

3. Screening and interviews

Approached candidates are screened in depth before they reach you. Good consultants interview personally, testing track record, motivation and leadership style, and they present a short, relevant shortlist rather than a pile of CVs.

4. References

References for senior hires should be taken seriously and taken early. The aim is to confirm what the candidate claims and to surface anything that would change your decision, before the offer rather than after it.

5. Offer and close

The final stage is as much about persuasion as administration. A search partner helps structure the offer, manages the negotiation and keeps both sides aligned so the preferred candidate actually signs.

Retained vs. contingency search — and when each makes sense

The two commercial models suit very different situations.

Contingency search

With contingency, the agency is paid only if a placement is made, and it may be working the role alongside other agencies. It suits mid-level and specialist roles where the candidate pool is broad and speed matters more than exclusivity. The trade-off is that effort is split across several roles and there is no guarantee of commitment.

Retained search

With retained search, you engage one firm exclusively and pay in stages — typically on commencement, at shortlist and on placement. It suits senior appointments where the pool is small, discretion matters and a failed hire is expensive. The retained model buys a dedicated consultant and a level of commitment that contingency cannot.

A simple rule: if the role can be filled from people who are actively looking, contingency is fine. If it depends on convincing people who are not looking, retained is the honest choice.

What to expect from a good search partner

A named lead

You should know exactly who is running your search, ideally one named consultant supported by a researcher rather than a rotating cast. Consistency of contact is part of the service.

Honest pushback on the brief

The most valuable thing a search partner does is tell you when the brief will not work: when the salary sits below market, when the requirements contradict each other, or when the timeline is unrealistic. A firm that simply agrees with everything is not doing its job.

A defined process

Ask how the search will run, how often you will hear from the consultant, what the shortlist will look like and what happens if the hire does not work out. A defined process and a clear guarantee separate a professional search from an expensive introduction.

What a hiring leader should prepare

A clear role scope

Know what the role owns, what it does not, and how it sits alongside the rest of the leadership team. Ambiguity here is the most common reason a senior search stalls.

Agreed success measures

Decide in advance what a good first year looks like — the outcomes rather than the responsibilities. It sharpens the brief and gives you something concrete to assess candidates against.

A realistic timeline

A senior search is not a same-week exercise. Mapping, approaching and assessing leaders takes time. A realistic timeline, agreed up front, keeps expectations aligned and stops the process being rushed at the wrong moment.
