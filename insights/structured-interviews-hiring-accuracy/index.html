Structured Interviews: How to Hire More Accurately

Structure is not a script that kills conversation. It is a scorecard, the same questions for every candidate, and a debrief about evidence rather than the loudest opinion in the room.

TIS Staffing

Recruitment Team

A structured interview is a hiring conversation built from three decisions made in advance: what the role has to achieve, which competencies you will test to find out whether someone can achieve it, and how each answer will be scored. Every candidate gets the same questions in the same order, interviewers score separately before discussing, and the debrief is about evidence rather than impressions. That is the whole method. It does not make hiring decisions for you, and it does not guarantee a good hire — but it removes the two things that quietly break most interview loops: candidates being asked different questions, and panel members agreeing with whoever speaks first.

What structure actually changes

An unstructured interview feels professional and produces very little usable evidence. Each interviewer wanders through their own favourite topics, candidates get asked things they happen to be good at, and the panel compares notes on different conversations. The result is a decision assembled from five unrelated impressions.

Structure changes what is comparable. If every candidate answers the same question about the same competency, the panel is no longer comparing people — it is comparing evidence about a specific capability. That is a far easier judgement, and it is the one you can defend afterwards if the hire goes wrong.

It also changes what the interview is for. In an unstructured loop the interview validates a feeling formed from the CV. In a structured loop the interview tests claims the CV made.

Start from a scorecard, not a question bank

The most common way this goes wrong is a team downloading a list of behavioural questions and calling it structure. A question bank asks the same things in the same order, which is a start, but it is not tied to the job. The scorecard comes first; the questions are derived from it.

Derive competencies from a definition of success

Write down what the person must have achieved twelve months in — the outcome, not the duties. Then ask what capabilities that outcome actually requires. A controller who has to rebuild a close process needs process design, stakeholder pushback and tolerance for a messy first quarter. An engineering lead taking over a struggling team needs prioritisation, technical judgement and the ability to say no to a director. Those are testable. "Strong communicator" is not.

This is the same discipline as writing the brief. If the brief never established what success looks like, the scorecard has nothing to hang on — which is why the two should be done together, in the order described in how to write a job description that attracts the right candidates.

Keep it to four or five competencies

A scorecard with twelve rows produces a panel that scores everything a three because nobody can hold twelve criteria in their head. Four or five competencies, each with a one-line definition of what strong evidence sounds like, is enough for most roles. If you cannot decide which five matter, the role definition is still unfinished.

Decide the rating scale before the first interview

Agree what 1, 3 and 5 mean. A workable version: 1 is no relevant evidence or an example that contradicts the competency; 3 is a credible example with visible judgement; 5 is a strong example plus a clear account of what the candidate would do differently. Anchors matter more than the number of points on the scale.

Writing questions that surface evidence

Two question types do most of the work, and they answer different questions.

Behavioural questions: what actually happened

Behavioural questions ask for a specific past event: "Tell me about the last time a delivery date slipped and you had to tell a client." Note the requirements — last time, a specific event, a named situation. Vague openings ("How do you handle pressure?") invite a philosophy, and a philosophy is unfalsifiable.

Write each question against one competency and only one. If a question could score three competencies, the panel will disagree about which box to tick.

Follow-ups that stop a rehearsed answer

Preparation is not dishonesty, and most candidates arrive with two or three polished stories. The follow-up is what separates a story from evidence: "What was your part specifically?", "What did the others want to do instead?", "What did you do when that did not work?", "What would you change now?" A candidate who did the thing can answer all four without leaving the same anecdote. A candidate who observed it from a distance usually cannot.

Do not rescue people out of a follow-up either. If an answer dissolves under questioning, that is the evidence, and it needs to be recorded rather than explained away in the debrief.

Situational questions for judgement

Situational questions describe a realistic scenario from the job and ask what they would do: "A month into the role, the finance director asks you to sign off numbers you believe are wrong. What happens next?" These are useful where the competency is judgement under real conditions rather than a track record, and they are especially useful for roles where nobody has held the exact title before.

Write situations from work your team has actually faced. Invented puzzles test whether someone is good at interviews.

Run the same loop for every candidate

Same questions, same order, same shape

If the first candidate gets a generous fifteen minutes on their strongest topic and the third gets a rushed ten, the scores are not comparable and the panel is really ranking interview timing. Keep the order fixed so fatigue affects everyone equally, and put the most important competency early rather than last.

Assign competencies, not whole conversations

Each interviewer should own two or three competencies and take the questions for those, rather than all interviewers asking everything. That produces depth instead of five shallow passes, and it stops the awkward moment where a candidate is asked the same question three times.

It also protects the candidate experience, which matters more than most panels assume. A loop where people are prepared, questions are relevant and time is respected reads as competence — the point made in why candidate experience wins offers, and often the reason a first-choice candidate accepts.

Record notes during, not after

Notes taken after the loop are reconstructed through the impression that formed during it. Write short factual notes as you go — what they said, what they did — and score within the hour, before memory smooths everything into a like or a dislike.

Score independently, then discuss

Why independent scoring matters

A debrief that opens with the most senior person's opinion is not a debrief, it is an announcement with witnesses. Once someone with authority has said "I thought they were strong", the quieter interviewer with a concrete concern about delivery will usually soften it.

So submissions go in before the conversation: each interviewer scores every competency and writes one line of evidence per score. Then the panel reads them together. Disagreement becomes the useful part of the meeting rather than something to be smoothed over.

When two interviewers score the same competency very differently, the fix is to return to the evidence. One of them usually heard something the other did not, and that gap is exactly what an unstructured process loses.

What a good debrief contains

For each competency: the scores, the evidence behind them, and the disagreements. Then the honest question — can we point to something this person actually did that suggests they will achieve the outcome we wrote down twelve months ago? Finally, what would we need to check before an offer, and what would we have to support in the first ninety days.

What it should not contain

No comparisons to a previous favourite candidate who is no longer in the process. No culture-fit verdicts based on shared hobbies. No discussion of a candidate's likeability dressed up as leadership evidence. And no re-litigating the questions — if the loop was wrong, fix it for the next role, not mid-decision.

When structure is overkill

Structure earns its cost when the role is expensive to get wrong and there are several candidates to compare. For a single internal move, a short contract extension or a role where the track record is already visible in the building, a full scorecard and panel can add ceremony without adding accuracy.

The honest version: use a scorecard and independent notes for any permanent hire you would be unhappy to re-run in six months. Scale the panel size, not the discipline.

Common mistakes

Downloading a generic question set with no scorecard behind it. Writing ten competencies and rating everything a three. Asking hypothetical questions for every competency, which rewards eloquence. Scoring after the debrief has already started. Letting the hiring manager speak first. Treating a strong answer to one question as evidence for the whole scorecard. And changing the questions mid-process after an early candidate does badly — which invalidates every comparison you have made so far.
