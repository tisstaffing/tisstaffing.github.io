How to Write a Job Description That Attracts the Right Candidates

A job description is a screening document and a sales document at once. A practical structure, how to split must-haves from nice-to-haves, and the mistakes that filter out the people you want.

TIS Staffing

Recruitment Team

Knowing how to write a job description means accepting that the advert has two jobs at once. It has to filter out people who cannot do the work, and it has to sell the role to the people who can. Get both right and the shortlist narrows itself. In practice: use a title people actually search for, open with a short honest summary of why the role exists, describe the real responsibilities in plain language, separate must-haves from nice-to-haves, then state the location, the compensation range and the interview process. Do that before the advert goes live, not after a weak shortlist arrives.

What a job description is actually for

Most job descriptions fail because they are written for the HR file rather than for the person deciding whether to apply. A duty list is comfortable to write and tells a candidate almost nothing they can act on.

A vague or inflated advert does real damage: it attracts people who like the sound of the words and repels the ones who can tell the difference. A precise one does the opposite. It reads as a company that knows what it is hiring for, which is the first signal a good candidate receives about what working there will be like.

A section-by-section structure

Job title

Use the title the market uses. If you would search for a Controller, do not advertise a Financial Operations Lead. Internal grades, invented titles and hybrid labels all hide the role from the people searching for it, and from search engines too.

A short, honest summary of the role and why it exists

One short paragraph answering a simple question: what is this person here to change? If the role is opening because someone left, say what needs picking up. If it is new, say what it is meant to fix or build. Candidates can picture that. They cannot picture "a key member of a dynamic team".

The actual responsibilities

Describe the work in plain language, in the order it happens. What does the first month look like, what does the steady state look like, and who does this person work with day to day? Six honest bullets beat twenty abstract ones.

Must-haves, kept short

Three to five things a candidate must already be able to do on day one. Everything else belongs in the next section, or nowhere.

Location and working arrangement

Say where the role sits, how many days are expected on site, what travel looks like and who the role reports to. These details decide whether someone applies at all, and getting them wrong wastes both sides time.

Compensation range and core benefits

Where it is appropriate to publish a range, publish it, and explain how bonus or commission sits on top. Legal requirements around pay disclosure vary by jurisdiction and change over time, so confirm your own obligations with a qualified professional rather than assuming a single rule applies everywhere.

The interview process and expected timeline

How many stages, what each one covers, who the candidate will meet and roughly how long it takes. Publishing it does two things: it respects the candidate time and it holds your own process to account. A described process you do not follow is worse than describing none, because the gap is the first thing a strong candidate notices — the point made in why candidate experience wins offers.

A simple how-to-apply step

One clear instruction, one destination. Tell them exactly what to send and whether a cover letter matters. Two competing application routes is one too many.

How to separate must-haves from nice-to-haves

Decide what the person must already have, and what your team is genuinely willing to teach. Then be honest about which list each item belongs on. A requirement that nobody would fail the role for is a nice-to-have wearing a must-have badge.

Three habits inflate requirement lists and quietly shrink the pool for the wrong reasons. Years-of-experience inflation: "eight years" when the work needs competence, not a calendar. Degree requirements that are not truly required, which filter out capable self-taught people. And long tool lists, where most of the tools can be learned in a fortnight by someone who understands the underlying work.

The cost is not abstract. A fifteen-requirement advert does not raise the bar; it filters on the wrong criteria and loses the candidate who meets twelve and would learn the rest quickly.

Describe the real scope, including the unglamorous parts

Say what the job actually involves, not the version you would put in a brochure. Month-end close includes chasing reconciliations. Payroll includes answering the awkward questions. Infrastructure work includes on-call.

Candidates who understand the unglamorous parts before they accept rarely resign over them later. Candidates who discover them in week three often do. Being specific about the hard parts is not a deterrent — it is the strongest retention decision available at the advert stage.

Plain, inclusive language

Cut internal jargon. "Leverage cross-functional synergies" means nothing outside your building, and coded wording about culture fit narrows who feels invited to apply. Keep sentences short. Say what the environment is actually like — pace, autonomy, how decisions get made — rather than asserting that it is fast-paced and collaborative, which is what every advert claims and therefore filters nobody.

Pay transparency, honestly handled

A published range saves screening time on both sides. It stops the calls with candidates whose expectations are a different order of magnitude, and it signals that the number is not a problem to be negotiated down later.

It is not a universal legal requirement, and the rules differ by where you hire. Treat disclosure as a decision to confirm against your own obligations with a qualified professional, then make the range as accurate as you can — a range you widen to cover everything is only slightly better than no range at all.

Decide the role before you write the advert

Every section above depends on decisions that have to be made before the writing starts: what the role owns, what the compensation range is, and what success looks like at twelve months. Most weak shortlists trace back to a brief that was never agreed — three stakeholders each holding a different version of the job.

That is the order TIS Staffing works in. Before a single advert goes live we run a position definition and alignment review with the hiring manager, agreeing the role, the compensation range and the definition of success. Only then is the advert written and the market approached. It is a slower first week and a considerably faster search.

Common job description mistakes

A laundry list of duties with no indication of which matter. "Rockstar" and "ninja" language, which signals a culture rather than a job. Hidden requirements — the real must-haves that only appear at interview. No salary information at all. A 2,000-word advert that nobody finishes. And copying a template from another company or another role, which produces a description of a job that does not exist.

A fill-in-the-blanks outline

Title, in the words the market uses. Why this role exists, in two sentences. What the person will own in their first year. Must-haves, three to five. Nice-to-haves, kept clearly separate. Where and how they will work. Compensation range and core benefits. The interview process and timeline. How to apply, in one step.

If you can fill all nine in without hedging, the advert is ready to write.

Frequently asked questions

Long enough to describe the work honestly and short enough that a good candidate finishes it. Most roles fit comfortably into one screen of summary, six to eight responsibilities and two short requirement lists. If the advert runs longer than a few minutes of reading, the extra length is usually repetition rather than information.
